# Research questions and contributions

## Problem

Mobile-money scams arrive as SMS that pose as relatives, agents, landlords or employers and ask the victim to send money; in Tanzania a common hook is *"ni tumie kwa namba hii"* (send it to me on this number). Published Swahili detectors report 98.7% (BongoScam) to 99.86% (Mambina et al., 2022) accuracy. WDYRI reproduces that result and then asks what prior work skipped:

1. **RQ1, leakage.** How much of the score survives when near-duplicate scam templates cannot appear in both train and test?
2. **RQ2, robustness.** How far does each model fall under rule-based obfuscation (lookalike letters, split words, English code-switching), and do simple defences recover it?
3. **RQ3, transfer.** Can a model trained only on Swahili catch real Chichewa fraud SMS from Malawi, and does Africa-centric pretraining (AfroXLMR) beat plain XLM-R there?

Answering them raised a fourth question, which became the project's main contribution: **what are these models actually reading?**

**Users:** mobile-money customers checking a suspicious SMS, and the telcos or fintechs that could run the check for them.

## Is the task already solved?

The rubric asks, for machine translation and text classification, for evidence that the chosen language is not already served by an established MT system. Swahili is served by established MT (the brief itself names it as barred for the MT option), and WDYRI does not claim otherwise. Its case rests on the task instead: an MT system does not classify messages, so what matters for a classification project is whether *this task in this language* is already solved by an established system. Because the rubric's wording leaves this open, and scam detection is not in the brief's list of example tasks, the topic needs the instructor's approval. We checked every Swahili scam-SMS detector we could find:

| Existing work | What exists | Why it does not settle the task |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mambina et al. (2022) | 99.86% accuracy, random forest | Data not public; scams under 1% of messages; no robustness or transfer test |
| Mambina et al. (2024) | Swahili SMS spam, 99.98% (CNN-LSTM, ten-fold CV) | Spam rather than scams; random folds only |
| BongoScam (Dioniz, 2024a, 2024b) | public data + Naive Bayes app, 98.7% | One random split; we show its scores rest partly on a phone-number shortcut (E3b, E10) |
| Njame et al. (2026) | F1 about 0.998 (five-fold CV) | Random folds only; reads stable ablations as a linguistic signal |
| Chiuseni et al. (2026) | obfuscation study | LLM-written attacks; defences proposed, not tested; English-Swahili only |
| Taylor & Robert (2025a) | Chichewa fraud SMS, about 97% | Classical models; no cross-language transfer |

No public, robustly evaluated Swahili scam classifier exists, and none for Chichewa beyond classical in-language models. Swahili is chosen because it has public, real-world scam SMS and the most published detectors to test against; Chichewa extends the question to a lower-resource neighbour that XLM-R never saw in pretraining.

## What is new

1. **A shortcut, found and measured.** In BongoScam, 86% of scams and 0% of genuine texts contain a phone number or link; the fixed rule "has a number" scores F1 0.92 on the whole dataset. Minimal-pair stress tests (E10) show the cost: appending a phone number to a genuine message makes fine-tuned AfroXLMR call it a scam **96%** of the time, and deleting the number from a scam makes it miss **20%**.
2. **A targeted fix.** Number-balanced counterfactual training (E11) deletes or appends placeholder tokens in 42% of training texts so that a number appears about equally often in both classes. AfroXLMR's stress-test false alarms fall from 96% to **0%** and its misses from 20% to **2%**; its test PR-AUC rises from 0.958 to 0.985 (the F1 gain, 0.78 to 0.82, is not significant, p = 0.06). The BiLSTM's false alarms fall from 28% to 0% too. The fix has costs: for AfroXLMR, lower Chichewa precision; for the char LR, weaker resistance to lookalike letters; for the BiLSTM, weaker resistance to code-switching and lower Chichewa F1; and the number-balanced AfroXLMR flags disguised genuine texts more often.
3. **Leakage-safe evaluation.** Template-disjoint splits, ten re-drawn splits per protocol, a per-template view, seed-pooled bootstrap intervals and paired significance tests, instead of one random split.
4. **Reproducible attacks with controls and a second attacker.** Three rule-based attacks at three intensities, two tested defences, control sets that disguise *genuine* texts the same way, a second attacker model, and a held-out lookalike attack showing that normalisation only undoes the characters it was written for. The controls show that the transformers' resistance to disguise is largely suspicion of odd-looking text: fully disguised genuine texts are flagged 54-93% of the time.
5. **A complementary ensemble, prompted by test errors, with members chosen on validation and checked out of sample.** A character n-gram model and AfroXLMR fail on different messages; flagging when either flags (both members number-balanced, chosen on validation stress tests) scores test F1 0.994 (level with the number-balanced char LR alone, 0.988; p = 0.37) and keeps 0.95-0.99 under lookalike and split-word attacks from either attacker (+0.18 to +0.23 over the char LR; p < 0.001 in the three comparisons tested). On two fresh splits it beats AfroXLMR by 0.20-0.22 (p < 0.001) and matches the char LR (+0.02-0.03, not significant). It is the deployed app.
6. **Swahili to Chichewa transfer.** Zero-shot transfer is weak: trained models reach fraud F1 0.46-0.75, and a 9-word length rule scores 0.72. AfroXLMR ranks Chichewa fraud better than XLM-R (PR-AUC +0.15, p < 0.001) and adapts faster from 50 examples (F1 0.87 vs 0.78, p < 0.001), but a char LR without pretraining adapts just as fast (0.88).

## Findings in one minute

* **E1:** the published 98.7% reproduces exactly (98.68% accuracy).
* **RQ1:** over 10 re-drawn splits, keeping templates out of the test costs character n-gram models 2.6-5.7 F1 points (char LR 0.998 → 0.972, char NB 0.995 → 0.938) and word models about 1 point or less; all four gaps are significant (Mann-Whitney p ≤ 0.02). The bigger effect is *which* scripts land in the test: one unseen landlord-impersonation script (31 of 81 test scams; no number, link or amount) is caught by word NB (31 of 31) and word LR (29 of 31) but not by the transformers or the BiLSTMs (0-13%), whose test F1 is 0.76-0.78 while their per-template F1 is 0.98-0.99.
* **RQ2:** lookalike letters cut word-level LR recall from 0.96 to 0.30; normalisation restores it to 0.95 (+0.52 F1, p < 0.001) at no cost on clean text, but only partly repairs split words, and against held-out lookalikes it was not written for it recovers nothing (word LR still loses 45% of its F1). The transformers lose nothing under disguise, but the controls show why: at full intensity they also flag disguised *genuine* texts (AfroXLMR 54-60%, XLM-R 93%), and the scams AfroXLMR catches only once disguised are mostly the landlord script it misses when clean. The char LR never flags disguised genuine texts but loses 21-24% of its F1. Results are the same with a second attacker. Code-switching is the attack normalisation cannot undo; adversarial training cuts the char LR's loss there from 19% to 4%.
* **RQ3:** zero-shot fraud F1 on 733 Chichewa SMS is 0.46-0.75 for trained models, against 0.68 for the phone rule and 0.72 for the length rule. AfroXLMR's ranking advantage over XLM-R is significant, but a BiLSTM over Swahili fastText vectors ranks as well (PR-AUC 0.80 vs 0.81), so African pretraining is not the only route. Twenty Chichewa examples lift char LR from 0.65 to 0.82 on the same messages.
* **The shortcut:** removing it changes what transfers. Number-balanced AfroXLMR keeps its Chichewa F1 (0.72) but loses precision (-0.06, p < 0.001); number-balanced char LR loses most of its transfer (0.65 → 0.15): for the classical models, "contains a number" *was* the transfer.

## Related work

**SMS spam and smishing detection.** Text-message spam filtering has long been treated as text classification; the SMS Spam Collection (Almeida et al., 2011) made English benchmarks standard, with Naive Bayes and support vector machines over word counts as strong baselines. Swahili smishing work follows the same recipe. Mambina et al. (2022) collected 32,259 Swahili messages and report 99.86% accuracy with TF-IDF features and a random forest, but, as Taylor & Robert (2025a) note, scams are under 1% of their data and the data are not public. BongoScam (Dioniz, 2024a, 2024b) released 1,508 labelled Tanzanian messages with a word-count Naive Bayes model at 98.7% accuracy. Njame et al. (2026) report F1 near 0.998 for a linear SVM and a random forest under five-fold cross-validation on 2,408 Tanzanian short messages, 1,377 of them real SMS from BongoScam, with no practical gain from a CNN; because performance held across four ablations, they conclude the signal is linguistic rather than an artefact of how the data were built. For Swahili SMS spam more broadly, Mambina et al. (2024) report 99.98% accuracy with a CNN-LSTM hybrid under ten-fold cross-validation on telecom data. All of these evaluate on random splits or random cross-validation of one dataset, so copies of one scam script can sit on both sides. For Chichewa, Taylor & Robert (2025a) built the fraud set used here and report about 97% accuracy with random forests and SVMs, again in-language and with classical models only.

**Leakage and evaluation design.** A single random split can overstate how well a model generalises. Gorman & Bedrick (2019) show that differences between systems reported on a standard split often do not hold on randomly drawn splits, and Søgaard et al. (2021) argue that random splits overestimate performance on new data and recommend harder, biased splits. Near-duplicates shared between training and test inflate scores further (Elangovan et al., 2021). Scam SMS are prone to this because a campaign reuses one script with a new name or number, so WDYRI groups such scripts into templates and keeps each template on one side of the split. Its ten re-drawn splits, bootstrap intervals and paired tests follow standard advice on significance testing in NLP (Dror et al., 2018), and its three seeds per neural model reflect how much fine-tuning varies with the random seed on small data (Dodge et al., 2020; Mosbach et al., 2021).

**Shortcuts and behavioural testing.** Models often succeed by exploiting artefacts of how a dataset was built rather than the intended signal: cue words in natural language inference hypotheses (Gururangan et al., 2018), syntactic heuristics (McCoy et al., 2019) and shortcut learning more broadly (Geirhos et al., 2020). Behavioural tests with minimal edits expose such failures where aggregate accuracy hides them (Ribeiro et al., 2020). Counterfactually augmented data, in which documents are minimally revised so that their label flips, makes models rely less on spurious features (Kaushik et al., 2020). BongoScam's phone numbers are such an artefact: E10 applies minimal-pair tests to it, and E11 adapts counterfactual augmentation by editing only the number token and keeping every label. This is also where WDYRI parts from Njame et al. (2026): stable ablations under random folds do not show what a model does when only the number changes, while a minimal pair holds the rest of the message fixed.

**Adversarial text and defences.** Small character-level changes break text classifiers: gradient-guided character flips (Ebrahimi et al., 2018), visually similar Unicode characters (Eger et al., 2019) and invisible or reordering characters (Boucher et al., 2022); word substitutions also degrade fine-tuned BERT (Jin et al., 2020). Proposed defences include a word-recognition front end that repairs adversarial misspellings before classification (Pruthi et al., 2019) and visual embeddings or adversarial training (Eger et al., 2019). For smishing, Chiuseni et al. (2026) found that classical detectors lost up to 98.8% of their F1 under obfuscation written by a large language model, while transformers lost at most 35.1%; they proposed defences but did not test them. WDYRI's attacks are rule-based so anyone can rerun them exactly, its normalisation defence is a simple front end in the spirit of Pruthi et al. (2019), and because that defence was written knowing the attack, a held-out set of lookalike characters tests whether it generalises.

**Cross-lingual transfer and African languages.** Most of the world's languages, including most African languages, have little labelled data or tooling (Joshi et al., 2020). Multilingual encoders transfer a classifier trained in one language to others without target-language labels (Pires et al., 2019; Wu & Dredze, 2019; Conneau et al., 2020), but zero-shot transfer is weakest for low-resource and distant target languages, and a handful of target-language examples closes much of the gap (Hu et al., 2020; Lauscher et al., 2020). Africa-centric models address the pretraining gap: AfriBERTa, pretrained on less than 1 GB of text in 11 African languages, is competitive with much larger multilingual models (Ogueji et al., 2021), and AfroXLMR continues XLM-R's pretraining on 17 African languages including Swahili and Chichewa (Alabi et al., 2022). XLM-R's pretraining covers Swahili but not Chichewa (Conneau et al., 2020), so Swahili-to-Chichewa transfer with both models also tests the value of target-language pretraining.

**The gap.** Each strand above has been studied, but not together for African-language scam SMS: published Swahili detectors stop at random splits or random cross-validation, the one robustness study used English-Swahili data and untested defences, and no earlier work tests scam detection across African languages. WDYRI combines leakage-safe evaluation, a measured shortcut and its fix, reproducible attacks with tested defences, and Swahili-to-Chichewa transfer.
